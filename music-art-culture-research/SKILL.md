# Music, Art & Culture Research

## Purpose

Provide a rigorous, reproducible research workflow for studying music, art, culture, media, and related social-science questions. Combine humanities interpretation with social-science methodological discipline. Prefer evidence over plausible-sounding synthesis, distinguish facts from interpretation, and make uncertainty explicit.

## Core principles

1. **Research question first** — clarify the object, period, geography, population/community, concepts, and desired type of answer before collecting evidence.
2. **Claim → Evidence → Source → Confidence** — every important factual claim should be traceable to supporting evidence and an appropriate source.
3. **Primary sources before secondary summaries** when the research question permits: recordings, scores, artworks, exhibition catalogues, interviews, archival documents, original datasets, institutional records, contemporaneous publications, and creators' own statements.
4. **Triangulate** — corroborate consequential claims across independent sources and explicitly flag disagreement.
5. **Method follows question** — do not force quantitative methods onto interpretive questions or qualitative methods onto questions requiring population-level estimates.
6. **Separate description, interpretation, and inference** — state what the source says, what it may mean, and what can reasonably be inferred as different layers.
7. **Reproducibility** — record search terms, databases, inclusion/exclusion criteria, dates searched, transformations, coding decisions, and analytical assumptions when they materially affect the result.
8. **Reflexivity** — identify positionality, selection effects, archival gaps, platform bias, survivorship bias, and other limitations when relevant.

## Research domains

### Music

Use concepts and methods from:
- musicology and historical musicology
- ethnomusicology
- popular music studies
- music sociology
- sound studies
- genre and scene studies
- music industry and political economy
- platform/streaming studies
- performance studies
- fan and audience studies

Possible objects include artists, albums, songs, performances, scenes, genres, instruments, technologies, labels, venues, festivals, platforms, audiences, and music-related institutions.

### Art and visual culture

Use concepts and methods from:
- art history
- visual culture studies
- contemporary art studies
- museum and exhibition studies
- iconography and iconology
- semiotics
- material and institutional analysis
- artist and artwork research
- provenance and archival research

Consider artwork, artist, exhibition, institution, patronage, reception, medium, historical context, and circulation rather than treating an artwork as an isolated object.

### Culture and society

Use concepts and methods from:
- cultural studies
- sociology
- anthropology
- media and communication studies
- cultural economy and creative industries
- subculture and scene research
- identity, class, race, gender, migration, and globalization as analytical categories where relevant to the research question
- popular culture and everyday-life studies

## Method selection

### Qualitative methods

Choose among:
- ethnography and participant observation
- semi-structured interviews and focus groups
- thematic analysis
- qualitative content analysis
- discourse analysis
- conversation analysis
- narrative analysis
- grounded theory
- interpretative phenomenological analysis
- case study research
- archival research
- comparative-historical analysis
- process tracing
- participatory/community-based research
- Delphi and Q methodology when appropriate

For qualitative projects, define the unit of analysis, sampling/recruitment logic, coding scheme, analytic procedure, reflexivity practices, and trustworthiness strategy. Where appropriate, consider member checking, negative cases, audit trails, inter-coder discussion, and triangulation.

### Quantitative and computational methods

Choose among:
- descriptive statistics and exploratory data analysis
- surveys and sampling analysis
- regression and causal inference
- randomized experiments and quasi-experiments
- difference-in-differences when assumptions are defensible
- regression diagnostics and robustness checks
- quantitative content analysis
- social network analysis
- bibliometrics/scientometrics
- computational cultural analysis and text analysis
- program/policy evaluation

Do not imply causality from correlation. State estimands, comparison groups, identification assumptions, missing-data treatment, uncertainty, and robustness checks when relevant.

## Literature research workflow

1. Translate the question into searchable concepts and synonyms.
2. Search scholarly indexes and bibliographic systems such as Google Scholar, JSTOR, Crossref, OpenAlex, library catalogues, and discipline-specific databases when available.
3. Start with high-quality reviews and landmark works, then trace references backward and citations forward.
4. Search for competing schools of thought, not only confirming evidence.
5. Build a literature matrix containing author/year, research question, object/sample, method, evidence, main findings, limitations, and relevance.
6. Distinguish systematic review, scoping/mapping review, narrative review, and ordinary background research.
7. Identify gaps only after checking whether the apparent gap is caused by search limitations, terminology differences, inaccessible archives, or disciplinary boundaries.
8. For systematic work, record databases, queries, date ranges, screening criteria, exclusion reasons, and search date.

## Artist, album, artwork, or exhibition research

Use a layered dossier:

1. **Identification** — canonical name, dates, aliases, medium/format, release/exhibition dates, location, relevant identifiers.
2. **Primary evidence** — recording, artwork, score, interview, exhibition materials, archival documents, creator statements, contemporaneous sources.
3. **Historical context** — institutions, scenes, technologies, political/economic context, contemporaneous reception.
4. **Secondary scholarship** — peer-reviewed articles, books, academic chapters, reputable criticism.
5. **Reception** — reviews, interviews, audience/fan discourse, charts or platform data where relevant.
6. **Interpretation** — connect formal features to historical/social arguments while marking interpretation as interpretation.
7. **Contradictions and gaps** — document disputed dates, authorship, attribution, provenance, or conflicting accounts.

Never present a promotional biography, fan wiki, or unsourced database entry as equivalent to scholarly or primary evidence.

## Cultural-phenomenon workflow

For a phenomenon such as a genre, scene, fandom, trend, ritual, platform practice, or cultural movement:

1. Define the phenomenon and competing definitions.
2. Establish chronology and geography.
3. Identify actors, institutions, infrastructures, technologies, and economic incentives.
4. Examine production, circulation, mediation, participation, and reception.
5. Compare insider/emic accounts with analytical/etic interpretations.
6. Look for counterexamples and marginalized or missing voices.
7. Test explanations against alternative mechanisms.
8. Synthesize micro-level practices with meso-level institutions and macro-level structures where the evidence supports doing so.

## Source evaluation hierarchy

Classify sources explicitly:

- **Primary** — original cultural object, archival record, interview, creator statement, contemporaneous document, original dataset, direct observation.
- **Scholarly secondary** — peer-reviewed article, academic monograph, scholarly chapter, dissertation, critical edition.
- **Institutional** — museum, archive, library, university, government, professional association, established cultural institution.
- **High-quality journalism/criticism** — useful for chronology, reception, interviews, and contemporary context; verify consequential claims where possible.
- **Tertiary/popular** — encyclopedias, databases, blogs, fan pages, aggregators; useful for discovery but generally not sufficient alone for important claims.

Evaluate authority, proximity to the event/object, methodological transparency, independence, date, incentives, editorial process, and corroboration.

## Evidence and citation protocol

For each major claim, internally track:

`Claim | Evidence | Source type | Source | Confidence | Caveat`

Use citations close to the claims they support. Do not cite a source merely because it discusses the same topic; verify that it actually supports the specific claim.

When sources disagree:
- state the disagreement;
- identify which sources are primary or closer to the evidence;
- explain methodological or contextual reasons for the discrepancy when supported;
- avoid false precision;
- retain uncertainty if it cannot be resolved.

## Analysis guardrails

- Do not fabricate citations, quotations, page numbers, archival identifiers, statistics, interviews, or release/exhibition details.
- Do not turn an interpretive reading into a historical fact.
- Do not infer authorial intent solely from the work unless supported by evidence.
- Do not equate popularity with cultural significance without defining the measure.
- Do not generalize from a small or convenience sample without qualification.
- Do not treat platform metrics as neutral measures of cultural value; account for recommendation systems, access, monetization, and measurement bias where relevant.
- Avoid presentism: interpret historical actors in their own context before applying contemporary categories.
- Avoid essentializing communities, genres, cultures, or identities.
- Distinguish correlation, association, mechanism, and causation.

## Research outputs

Adapt the final format to the task. Useful outputs include:
- concise evidence-backed answer
- annotated bibliography
- literature matrix
- systematic/scoping review protocol
- artist/album/artwork dossier
- exhibition or institution research brief
- cultural phenomenon case study
- comparative analysis
- qualitative coding framework
- quantitative analysis plan
- source/evidence audit
- research memo with open questions and next steps

For substantial research, end with:
1. key findings;
2. strongest evidence;
3. competing interpretations;
4. limitations and evidence gaps;
5. recommended next research steps.

## Quality gate

Before finalizing substantial research, check:

- [ ] The research question is explicit.
- [ ] Key terms and scope are defined.
- [ ] Important factual claims have appropriate evidence.
- [ ] Primary and secondary sources are distinguished.
- [ ] Sources are sufficiently independent and triangulated.
- [ ] Method matches the research question.
- [ ] Causal language is justified.
- [ ] Interpretations are marked as interpretations.
- [ ] Counterevidence and uncertainty are addressed.
- [ ] Search/research limitations are disclosed.
- [ ] No citations, quotations, or facts were invented.
- [ ] The conclusion does not exceed what the evidence supports.

## Suggested research sequence

**Discover → Define → Search → Screen → Trace → Extract → Evaluate → Analyze → Triangulate → Synthesize → Cite → Audit**

When the task is exploratory, prioritize breadth first. When the task is scholarly or publication-oriented, prioritize transparent methods, source quality, reproducibility, and explicit limitations.
